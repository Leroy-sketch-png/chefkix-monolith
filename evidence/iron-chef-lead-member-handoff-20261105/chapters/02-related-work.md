## 2.1 Ingredient substitution benchmarks and graph ranking

Ingredient substitution is not one uniform prediction problem. A system may retrieve an ingredient associated with a target in a corpus, rank substitutes conditioned on a recipe, propose a change to a recipe's ingredient list, or rewrite the instructions after a change. These tasks differ in their input, candidate object, target, and unit of evaluation. Their results cannot be compared by a shared label such as “substitution accuracy” without checking those contracts.

Fatemi et al. introduce the Recipe1MSubs benchmark and GISMo, a graph-based ingredient-substitution model that combines recipe context with ingredient relations to rank plausible substitutions [S01]. The source paper reports standardized splits, metrics, and baselines. GISMo is therefore the central graph-ranking comparator for this project, but reproducing its score requires matching its split, identity mapping, graph, candidate construction, and metric implementation—not simply querying a similarly named model. The active evidence ledger records a pinned GISMo-v23 source/data preparation path and explicit identity checks; those local structural checks do not establish that every historical paper result has been independently reproduced.

The subsequent Senath et al. work applies supervised fine-tuning and direct preference optimization to language-model ingredient substitution and reports a Hit@1 result of 22.04% for its best stated setup [S02]. Its task emits a single substitute, whereas GISMo ranks a candidate list. The recorded full-text screen also found that Senath uses the same 49,044/10,729/10,747 Recipe1MSubs train/validation/test split as GISMo and selects SFT checkpoints by validation loss. The published value remains author-reported here, not independently reproduced. The split/checkpoint facts make reuse of that validation split a diagnostic comparison rather than an independent test of a validation-selected adapter; Chapter 5 gives the project's bounded run and its own claim boundary.

These benchmark and model papers establish meaningful precedents for corpus-based ranking and language-model generation. They do not, by themselves, establish that a single recorded gold exhausts the set of acceptable substitutes, nor that exact-match Hit@k estimates human-perceived culinary validity. Those are questions about the adequacy of the target and evaluation contract, not grounds to dismiss the benchmark or its reported score.

## 2.2 Recipe-context editing and generated instructions

Several systems move beyond predicting an isolated ingredient. SHARE performs dietary-constrained ingredient substitution and then generates recipe instructions from the edited ingredient set; its paper describes an 83K-pair RecipePairs corpus spanning seven dietary constraints, human evaluation, and a small real-world cooking study [S64]. RecipeCrit similarly supports recipe editing through iterative critique and instruction rewriting [S61]. Counterfactual recipe generation studies how a principal-ingredient change affects both ingredient mentions and cooking actions [S63]. These are close system-level precedents: they show that a usable recipe edit can require coordinated changes to ingredients and directions, and that recipe-level human assessment is a distinct outcome from pair retrieval.

The evaluations are not interchangeable with a blinded, shared candidate-pool assessment of each proposed substitute. SHARE's whole-recipe constraint-violation review and cooking trial, RecipeCrit's recipe-editing assessment, and counterfactual generation's action-aware evaluation answer different questions from whether a particular candidate is valid for a fixed source ingredient and recipe context. Conversely, a pair-level Hit@k result does not establish that the instructions remain executable or that the resulting dish is acceptable. The thesis must keep the prediction object and outcome explicit rather than treating one evaluation as a proxy for the others.

## 2.3 Dietary and allergen-aware recommendation

Prior work also connects substitutions to dietary goals and allergy-related constraints. The source inventory includes context-aware or restriction-aware recommendation, recipe adaptation, ontology work, and allergen-aware recipe ranking [S33, S40–S43]. The depth of review differs: some entries have publisher full-text screening, some only an abstract or metadata, and some are linked versions of the same research lineage. The current strategy's source-by-source notes preserve these differences and identify unresolved artifact, data, and case-lineage questions.

This literature rules out broad claims that ingredient replacement, recipe-context substitution, dietary filtering, or human-rated recipe adaptation are new. It does not license a safety claim. A recommendation paper that filters or ranks recipes, an ontology that can represent restrictions, and a substitution model that mentions allergens do not establish clinically reliable allergen avoidance. Allergy safety requires authoritative constraint sources, exact ingredient/recipe interpretation, protocolized testing, and appropriate review. The active plan treats such results as a separate, gated contribution; no validation-set Hit@k score in Chapter 5 substitutes for that evidence.

## 2.4 Candidate-set validity, human review, and selective prediction

The project's proposed human audit concerns the validity of candidates under a specified context, including candidates absent from a single recorded reference. This question has neighboring precedents in recipe-editing human evaluations and broader recommender evaluation. Those precedents constrain novelty language but do not settle the exact estimand proposed here: a blinded, common candidate slate; independent candidate-level judgments; explicit treatment of multiple valid answers; and stratification by pair novelty or evidence coverage. The current protocol remains subject to unresolved freeze, reviewer, ethics, and adjudication gates. It must not be presented as an approved or completed study.

Conformal recommendation and reliability-controlled ranking are also established areas [S06–S10, S35–S39]. Their existence makes generic claims such as “the first reliable recommender” or “the first conformal method for recommendations” untenable. Their targets vary—from rating or preference prediction to slate-level error control—and should not be equated automatically with selective validity of culinary substitutes, allergen-specific error, or an abstention decision under sparse evidence. The contribution, if supported by future results, must be stated at the exact task-and-outcome level and compared to the closest method, not inferred from the absence of an identical title.

Likewise, an LLM judge is not ground truth by virtue of producing a confidence or rationale. Agreement with independent human labels, calibration design, subgroup behavior, disagreement handling, and the cost of an unsafe false acceptance remain empirical requirements. Under Contribution C3, the empirical descriptive calibration demonstrated that exchangeability cannot be taken for granted: while seen pairs permit informative sets ($\hat{t}=7$ at $\alpha=0.30$), unconditioned and unseen pairs result in vacuous sets ($\hat{t}=\infty$), confirming the methodological risk of assuming uniform calibration across culinary tasks without stratified guarantees.

## 2.5 Synthesis: a bounded research question, not a novelty claim

The reviewed families support four distinctions that organize the thesis:

1. **Pair retrieval versus recipe-conditioned candidate validity.** A corpus pair or rank metric measures relation to recorded targets; a shared slate can ask whether any proposed option is acceptable under a stated context.
2. **Ingredient prediction versus complete recipe editing.** Changing an ingredient may require changing quantities, preparation, or instructions; candidate correctness and whole-recipe executability need separate evidence.
3. **Dietary relevance versus allergen safety.** Constraint-aware ranking or a representational schema is not clinical or operational safety validation.
4. **Score or confidence versus selective reliability.** A ranked score, LLM rationale, or conformal set requires an outcome definition and human/label evidence appropriate to its claimed guarantee.

These distinctions motivate, but do not prove, the thesis direction: audit how pair novelty and evidence coverage affect single-gold benchmark interpretation; assess candidates in a shared, context-bearing evaluation; and investigate an explicit abstention/guardrail design only after its protocol and safety boundaries are frozen. The exact primary hypotheses, estimands, thresholds, and acceptance rules remain those in the active strategy, not newly chosen in this chapter.

The strongest alternative interpretation is that the proposed evaluation merely repackages existing recipe-editing human studies or generic recommender reliability methods. The disconfirming evidence would be a prior study using the same candidate object, common-pool blinding, context/novelty estimand, and outcome with adequate protocol similarity—or, for a claim of practical value, results showing no meaningful difference from simpler baselines. The current source map has already identified close prior art and narrowed broad claims, but it is not an exhaustive search. L42's full-text, artifact-lineage, overlap, and independent-review gates remain open; this chapter makes no “first,” “novel,” or “safe” claim.
